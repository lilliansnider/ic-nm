Identity Capital: Understanding Personal and Social Resources

Modified

October 14, 2026

The Identity Capital Model (ICM), developed by sociologist James E. Côté provides a framework for understanding the process of young adults finding their place in an increasingly individualistic, complex, and chaotic post-modern world. The model examines how people develop and use personal and social resources as they construct their identities and navigate the opportunities and constraints of their social environments (Côté, 1996, 1997). Through this perspective, identity development involves both the resources individuals acquire and the ways they use those resources to pursue personally meaningful goals.

Defining Identity Capital

Identity capital refers to the resources individuals develop and draw upon as they navigate life transitions. Côté’s (1996, 1997) model emphasizes the interaction between individual agency and the social contexts in which identity development occurs, recognizing that although individuals make decisions about who they want to become, their choices are shaped by the opportunities, expectations, and constraints within their environments. Côté and Levine (2016) further conceptualize identity capital in terms of the personal capacities and social resources that individuals accumulate and use to navigate their lives, including psychological strengths, interpersonal skills, knowledge, experiences, and relationships. These resources can influence how individuals approach decisions, respond to challenges, and pursue educational, professional, and personal goals. Within higher education, identity capital provides a useful framework for understanding how students’ experiences contribute to their developing identities and perceptions of future possibilities. Students enter college with varying levels of familiarity with academic institutions, professional expectations, and available opportunities, and their experiences throughout college can expand these resources through interactions with faculty and peers, the acquisition of new knowledge, and participation in academic and professional activities. Consequently, identity capital highlights how students’ developing identities are shaped by the resources they bring to college, the experiences they encounter, and the social environments that influence their opportunities to acquire and use these resources.

Tangible and Intangible Resources

The Identity Capital Model encompasses both tangible and intangible resources that influence the navigation and decision-making dilemmas that were mentioned above (Côté, 1997; Côté & Levine, 2016). Tangible resources relate to the qualifications, experiences, and connections individuals can draw upon, whereas intangible resources involve the psychological capacities that support personal agency, self-understanding, and decision-making. Although these categories represent different aspects of identity capital, they work together to influence how individuals pursue opportunities and navigate identity development.

Tangible Resources

Tangible identity capital encompasses resources that individuals can use to access opportunities, navigate social environments, and demonstrate their qualifications or experience. In higher education, these resources may include:

  • Education and credentials: Degrees, certifications, and other qualifications that demonstrate acquired knowledge or experience.
  • Social and professional connections: Relationships that provide access to information, guidance, mentorship, and opportunities.
  • Knowledge and experience: Familiarity with academic expectations, professional environments, and the processes involved in pursuing educational or career goals.
  • Communication and interpersonal skills: Abilities that support collaboration, relationship development, and effective communication across social settings.

These resources can shape students’ experiences by influencing the opportunities available to them and their ability to recognize and pursue those opportunities. For example, a student who develops a relationship with a professor may learn about an undergraduate research position, gain practical experience working with a research team, and establish additional professional connections. The initial relationship can provide access to an opportunity, while the resulting experience may expand the student’s knowledge, skills, and professional network. This example illustrates how resources can accumulate and create further opportunities over time. However, the value of tangible resources depends partly on the social environments in which individuals use them. A credential, skill, or professional connection may be more useful in some settings than others, depending on the expectations and opportunities within those environments. Identity capital therefore involves more than the resources individuals possess; it also concerns how those resources interact with the social contexts in which identity development takes place (Côté & Levine, 2016).

Intangible Resources

Whereas tangible resources can provide access to opportunities or demonstrate qualifications, intangible identity capital encompasses psychological capacities that support individuals in making decisions, pursuing goals, and navigating their environments. Côté’s work draws attention to personal resources associated with agency and identity development, including self-esteem, a sense of purpose, an internal locus of control, and capacities for reflection and decision-making (Côté, 1996, 1997; Côté & Levine, 2016). In the context of higher education, these resources may be reflected in:

  • Self-confidence: Confidence in one’s abilities and capacity to pursue goals.
  • Sense of purpose: An understanding of personal priorities and the goals that provide direction.
  • Agency: A perceived capacity to make decisions and take action within existing circumstances.
  • Critical thinking: The ability to evaluate information, consider alternatives, and make informed decisions.
  • Self-understanding: An awareness of personal experiences, values, interests, strengths, and developing identity.

Intangible resources can influence how students interpret their experiences and respond to opportunities. For example, a student may have access to an undergraduate research position but feel uncertain about their qualifications or ability to succeed. Confidence in their capacity to learn, a clearer understanding of their academic interests, and a willingness to seek guidance may help them pursue the position despite that uncertainty. In this way, psychological resources can influence whether students act on opportunities available to them and how they use the experiences that follow.

Tangible and intangible resources are therefore best understood as interconnected aspects of identity capital. Psychological capacities can influence how individuals seek out relationships, develop skills, and pursue opportunities, while educational experiences and social interactions can strengthen their knowledge, confidence, and sense of direction. Examining both types of resources provides a broader understanding of how students develop the capacities and experiences they draw upon as they navigate higher education and form identities connected to their academic, professional, and personal futures (Côté, 1997; Côté & Levine, 2016).

How Identity Capital Develops in College

Identity capital develops over time as individuals draw upon existing resources, engage with new experiences, and acquire additional knowledge, skills, relationships, and personal capacities. Côté’s (1997) research on university students examined identity capital resources and their relationship with subsequent resource acquisition, providing a basis for considering how resources may develop during the transition into higher education. College provides opportunities for this development both inside and outside the classroom. Academic projects, employment, volunteering, campus involvement, personal relationships, and other life experiences can contribute to students’ knowledge, practical skills, social connections, and self-understanding. For example, participating in a research lab may help a student develop technical skills, collaborate with others, and explore whether research aligns with their future goals. The experience may also expand their professional connections and strengthen their confidence in navigating unfamiliar academic environments.

Every student enters college with different experiences and resources, and their development does not follow a single path. Recognizing how resources accumulate over time helps explain how experiences across multiple areas of life can contribute to students’ developing identities and future possibilities.

Social Context and Access to Resources

Although individuals develop and use identity capital through their experiences and decisions, their opportunities to acquire resources are shaped by their social environments. Family responsibilities, financial circumstances, institutional practices, and access to information and social networks can influence how easily students navigate higher education. Bourdieu’s (1986) work on social and cultural capital highlights how access to valued knowledge, relationships, and institutional opportunities is unevenly distributed. For example, one student may enter college already familiar with undergraduate research opportunities, while another may need to learn how to find positions and approach faculty members. Differences in access to information can influence how students recognize and pursue opportunities, even when they share similar interests and abilities. It is equally important to recognize that students bring resources from experiences beyond formal education. Employment, caregiving, community involvement, and personal responsibilities can contribute practical knowledge, interpersonal skills, and problem-solving abilities. Identity capital provides a way to recognize these existing resources while considering how social environments shape opportunities for further development.

Recognizing Your Identity Capital

Reflecting on identity capital involves identifying the resources you have developed, understanding how your experiences have shaped them, and considering how they might support your future goals.

Consider the following questions: 1. What skills, experiences, or relationships have helped you navigate an important transition?
2. What personal strengths have you developed through challenges or unfamiliar experiences?
3. What resources could help you pursue a goal that matters to you?
4. What opportunities would you like to explore, and what might help you access them?
